# Background and Related Work

## The balagha inventory and classical Arabic poetry corpus context

Classical Arabic rhetoric, or *balagha*, is not a loose critical vocabulary but a formally codified system with a long scholastic history. Al-Jurjani's theory of poetic "wonder," most fully developed in his *Asrar al-Balagha*, marks a shift in classical literary judgment from scoring poetry for truthfulness and naturalness to judging it by its capacity to evoke wonder — novelty, strangeness, the unexpected — in the listener, an aesthetic al-Jurjani and later critics including al-Sakkaki treat as built into the structure of rhetorical figures themselves (Harb, 2020). Al-Sakkaki's *Miftah al-'Ulum* begins the tripartite division of the discipline into *ma'ani*, *bayan*, and *badi'*, later completed by al-Qazwini, that still structures how the tradition's rhetorical devices are catalogued (Fontana, 2025). The twenty-element inventory evaluated in this paper is drawn from exactly this lineage, by way of a modern synthesis (Cachia, 1998) rather than from a direct reading of the medieval sources. Citing Harb and Fontana here is a deliberate choice: both are reliable, modern, English-language scholarly anchors for a tradition this paper cannot responsibly cite only through its own medieval primary texts.

Against this centuries-deep rhetorical scholarship, the natural-language-processing side of classical Arabic poetry is comparatively young, and lopsided in what it has modeled. Qarah's AraPoemBERT (Qarah, 2024) pretrains a transformer on 2.09 million verses of Arabic poetry and reports strong results across five tasks — meter, sub-meter, and rhyme classification, poet-gender classification, and poem-level sentiment analysis — the first four of which are *formal* features recoverable in large part from surface phonology and morphology. No comparable large-scale resource exists yet for the *rhetorical* or *semantic* layer of the same poetry. That asymmetry is not incidental to this paper's argument: it is one more piece of evidence that the elements this paper studies resist the kind of surface pattern-matching that has already worked well for meter and rhyme.

## Prior automatic approaches to rhetorical-element identification

The direct predecessor this paper extends is Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik's rule-based system for identifying rhetorical elements in the same corpus of classical Arabic poems (Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik, 2023). Working from string and positional matching, a morphological analyzer, and a purpose-built sentiment and antonym lexicon, their system reaches an overall precision of 0.918 and recall of 0.902 across twenty rhetorical-element categories — a genuinely strong result for a rule-based pipeline. But the result is strong only because of what the system was permitted to leave out: Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik (2023) explicitly exclude metaphor (استعارة) and periphrasis (كناية) from automatic detection, describing them as too semantically demanding for rule-based matching to handle responsibly. This paper exists to take up exactly the elements its predecessor named as out of reach for its own method, and is the paper against which this study's own per-element results must be read.

## Digital-humanities precedents for annotation-category development and difficulty

A recurring argument in *Digital Humanities Quarterly*'s own methodological literature is that category difficulty should be measured empirically, not assumed from a category's apparent simplicity. Bender et al.'s study of discourse-referencing annotation in German parliamentary speech is the clearest statement of this point: the *coarsest*, apparently simplest binary judgment in their scheme returned the *lowest* inter-annotator agreement of any task they tested, a pattern the authors trace to the way even "simple" sentence-level judgments still depend on contextual knowledge the annotators do not uniformly share (Bender et al., 2023). This is a direct structural parallel to this project's own pilot work on periphrasis, where small-sample noise and run-to-run instability have already produced a misleadingly large apparent effect that halved on replication — the same lesson Bender et al.'s finding points to: difficulty must be measured, not read off a category's surface description.

Two further DHQ case studies model treating ambiguity itself as data rather than as noise to be resolved away. Zirker and Göggelmann's annotation of the modal verb "must" in Austen's *Emma* adopts a "maximal annotation" strategy — tagging every plausible reading of an ambiguous modal rather than forcing a single disambiguated answer — and report Fleiss's kappa separately for each annotated dimension, finding that modality itself is the hardest dimension to agree on (Zirker and Göggelmann, 2023). Münz-Manor and Marienberg-Milikowsky, working with a corpus of early Hebrew liturgical poetry annotated for metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, and simile, argue against collapsing multiple legitimate annotator readings into a single gold standard at all, preferring instead to visualize the range of readings a figurative-device tagset can support (Münz-Manor and Marienberg-Milikowsky, 2023). Both studies model, for a cognate or comparably figurative textual domain, the premise this paper's own difficulty-stratified evaluation design depends on: that "challenging" is something an evaluation can measure, not merely assert.

Gutman's network analysis of figurative language in *Timon of Athens* closes this part of the argument by naming the field's own next step. Gutman (2024) argues that figurative language is harder for computational methods than literal language — his own topic-classification model reaches only 72.94% accuracy on a bottom-up topic inventory built from manually extracted figurative instances — and proposes large language models (Llama, Gemini, ChatGPT) as untested future work for exactly this kind of classification task. The present paper is, in a precise sense, that proposed future work, carried out for a different literary tradition and language.

## LLM-in-DH literature: methodological framing

Two recent DHQ articles supply methodological and tonal models for how this kind of model-versus-human comparison should be conducted and reported. Tarkka et al.'s comparison of a BERT-based classifier against human questionnaire responses on Finnish parliamentary speech is the closest DHQ precedent in genre to this paper's own design: a rigorously reported, recent model-versus-human study pairing party-affiliation F1 reporting with an explanation-analysis layer (SHAP) showing which textual cues the model relies on, and finding that the model tracks stylistic and rhetorical cues human readers largely miss (Tarkka et al., 2026). This paper's Results and Discussion sections are structured along a similar split, for an analogous reason: an F1 table alone does not say *why* a model succeeds or fails on a given rhetorical element, and DHQ readers reasonably expect that question addressed directly.

Dobson's essay on interpretable machine learning in the humanities supplies the second, cautionary half of this framing. Dobson (2021) warns that accuracy figures and confidence scores can be "rhetorically structured" to look more decisive than the underlying evidence warrants, and argues for a cautious vocabulary when describing what a high score does, and does not, demonstrate. That caution is directly relevant to this paper's interpretation of its highest-scoring rhetorical elements, discussed further below.

## LLM benchmarks on figurative and rhetorical language beyond digital humanities

Outside DHQ, a substantial and rapidly growing non-DH literature already benchmarks large language models on metaphor and figurative-language identification, with more methodological machinery than anything reviewed so far. Fuoli et al. compare retrieval-augmented generation, prompt engineering, and fine-tuning across ten models on full-text (not pre-segmented) metaphor identification, reporting precision, recall, and F1 alongside a taxonomy of human–model discrepancies, and reach a best F1 of 0.79 with a fine-tuned model (Fuoli et al., 2025). This is the closest methodological template to the evaluation design adopted here: span-level identification over running text, evaluated against expert annotation, with discrepancies read as theoretically meaningful rather than undifferentiated noise.

That optimism needs a counterweight. Reimann and Scheffler test zero- and few-shot large language models on an extended metaphor-detection task and report a largely negative result, with one model showing severe overgeneralization (Reimann and Scheffler, 2025). Read alongside Fuoli et al. (2025), the contrast is itself the finding: metaphor- and figure-identification performance depends heavily on *how* a model is deployed — full-text context and a carefully engineered prompt or fine-tuning regime, rather than a bare zero-shot query — a methodological lesson this paper's own two-stage prompting design takes seriously.

A smaller but closely related strand of work moves past a flat "metaphor: yes or no" label toward classifying *named* rhetorical figures against a human-annotated scheme, the task this paper undertakes for classical Arabic balagha categories. Balestrucci et al. fine-tune open-weight multilingual models to classify eight named rhetorical figures — among them hyperbole, euphemism, and oxymoron — in ironic Italian text, and report human–model agreement using Krippendorff's alpha of 0.21 to 0.26, markedly below the 0.60 to 0.65 agreement between human annotators on the same figures (Balestrucci et al., 2025), a concrete benchmark this paper's own agreement figures can be read against. Hicke and Kristensen-McLachlan operationalize a single metaphor-identification procedure as a prompt for GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-4o, finding that these models reproduce the procedure's reasoning steps with what the authors describe as surprising depth (Hicke and Kristensen-McLachlan, 2024) — a second multi-model comparison on a procedurally defined task, and a plausible template for prompt design.

A caution belongs alongside these benchmarks. Ye et al. probe large language models' behavior during metaphor processing and argue that strong behavioral accuracy on a metaphor task can reflect shallow lexical or syntactic heuristics rather than robust semantic understanding of the figure itself (Ye et al., 2026). This is a necessary qualification for any paper, including this one, tempted to read a high identification score as evidence of genuine interpretive competence.

Finally, two companion surveys by Kühn, Mitrović and collaborators establish that the gap this paper addresses is structural, not only Arabic-specific. Reviewing thirty-nine papers and eighty-six detection approaches across eight languages — none of them Arabic — Kühn, Mitrović and Granitzer (2024) find that named rhetorical figures *beyond* metaphor, irony, and sarcasm (among them antithesis, a figure this paper's own corpus also evaluates) remain a generally under-studied sub-field, with nearly seventy percent of surveyed approaches still rule-based, and existing rhetorical ontologies essentially unused by any system reviewed. A companion short paper distills this review into ten recurring challenges facing computational detection of such figures, among them inconsistent cross-source definitions, boundary and intentionality ambiguity, and chronic dataset scarcity (Kühn and Mitrović, 2024). Neither paper covers periphrasis specifically, and neither touches Arabic; together they explain why periphrasis and metaphor — rather than antithesis, which Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik's (2023) rule-based system already handles reasonably well — were the elements that paper's authors judged out of reach for rule-based matching, and supply a checklist against which this paper's own difficulty findings can later be compared.

## LLMs on Arabic figurative and cultural language specifically

Two existing studies bring large language models to bear on Arabic figurative language specifically, and each falls short of this paper's exact target in a precisely nameable way. Al Kaabi et al. (2024) compare ChatGPT's interpretation of Arabic rhetorical figures — metaphor, simile, and metonymy among them — against the classical hermeneutical tradition associated with al-Jurjani, working through eight hand-selected example sentences. Their finding, that ChatGPT produces surface-level readings while missing the cultural and psychological depth a trained human reader supplies, is a genuinely useful qualitative signal, but the study is a single-model, small-sample exercise on isolated sentences rather than a poem corpus, with no benchmark metrics, no held-out test set, and no comparison across models. It is cited here, as this section's argument requires, strictly as a small qualitative study of this kind; its own literature review is not relied upon.

Attia et al.'s Kinayat benchmark (Attia et al., 2026) is, by contrast, methodologically rigorous: twenty-two open- and closed-source large language models are evaluated on Egyptian Arabic idioms and multidialectal Arabic proverbs across comprehension, pragmatic-use, and connotation-inference tasks, with quantified human-agreement figures reported throughout — the strongest model reaches at most 85.58% agreement with human annotators even on connotation items where every annotator had already agreed. This is the closest precedent found to a genuinely multi-model, quantitatively benchmarked study of Arabic figurative language — but of a different object than the one this paper examines. Kinayat evaluates modern colloquial idioms and proverbs — closed-form tasks such as multiple-choice comprehension, fill-in-the-blank completion, and connotation labeling — framed as cultural and pragmatic competence, not the identification of named classical rhetorical categories within a running verse of poetry. It contains no taxonomy of antithesis, periphrasis, metaphor, or metonymy as such, no stratification of difficulty by rhetorical-element type, and no span-level identification task of the kind Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik (2023) and the present study both center on. Read together, Al Kaabi et al. (2024) and Attia et al. (2026) show that the field already has both the motivating intuition that large language models handle Arabic figurative language imperfectly, and the methodological machinery to measure that imperfection rigorously — but neither study has yet pointed toward classical Arabic poetry or the named balagha inventory this paper evaluates.

## Foundational and theoretical references

A small number of standard references are held in reserve here rather than argued at length, to be drawn on as needed elsewhere in this paper. Lakoff and Johnson's Conceptual Metaphor Theory (Lakoff and Johnson, 1980) is the default theoretical anchor should the discussion of استعارة (metaphor) require one. Cohen's original statistic for inter-rater agreement on nominal categories (Cohen, 1960) and Hayes and Krippendorff's operational treatment of Krippendorff's alpha (Hayes and Krippendorff, 2007) are the two candidate sources for whichever inter-annotator-agreement measure this paper's annotation discussion ultimately reports.

## The gap this paper addresses

Automatic identification of rhetorical elements in classical Arabic poetry has so far relied on rule-based systems, which Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik (2023) show can reach high precision and recall overall — but only by excluding metaphor and periphrasis as too semantically demanding for rule-based matching. Large language models are a natural candidate for this gap, and two adjacent literatures show the ingredients exist separately: rigorous multi-model benchmarking of named rhetorical figures beyond metaphor and irony (Kühn, Mitrović and Granitzer, 2024), and a twenty-two-model, quantified benchmark of large language models on Arabic figurative language (Attia et al., 2026) — but for modern colloquial idioms and proverbs, not classical poetry or named balagha categories. No existing study evaluates multiple large language models on the identification of named classical Arabic rhetorical elements, including the semantically demanding cases rule-based systems cannot handle, against expert-annotated ground truth with a span-level, difficulty-stratified evaluation.

This gap motivates three research questions. **RQ1**: How accurately can current large language models identify named classical Arabic rhetorical elements (antithesis/طباق, periphrasis/كناية, metaphor/استعارة, metonymy/مجاز مرسل) in classical Arabic poetry, measured at the span level against expert-annotated ground truth? **RQ2**: Does LLM identification accuracy vary systematically by rhetorical-element type, and in particular, do the elements that Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik (2023) excluded as too semantic for rule-based detection (metaphor, periphrasis) show a different accuracy and error profile than the more surface-marked elements, such as antithesis, their rule-based system already handles well? **RQ3**: Does evaluating multiple large language models, rather than a single model as in Al Kaabi et al. (2024), reveal consistent cross-model difficulty patterns by element, or does difficulty vary idiosyncratically by model — and how do any such patterns compare to the general difficulty findings reported for non-Arabic, non-classical rhetorical-figure detection (Kühn, Mitrović and Granitzer, 2024) and for Arabic figurative language more broadly (Attia et al., 2026)?
