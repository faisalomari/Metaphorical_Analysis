# Reviewer Simulation — `paper_trimmed.md`

Two simulated DHQ peer reviewers, reading only `writing/paper_draft/final/paper_trimmed.md` as it currently stands (plus `open_items.md`, the DHQ format guide, and `reviewer_risks.md` for venue-fit context). Every objection below cites the actual paragraph, table, or line it is reading. No fact about the paper is invented; where a reviewer's objection rests on something the paper itself has not yet done (e.g., a missing significance test), that is stated as an absence, not attributed to a specific sentence.

---

## Reviewer 1 — Digital Humanities / Arabic literature scholar

### How this reviewer understands the paper

A paper that picks up exactly where a 2023 DHQ article (Abd Alhadi, Hussein and Kuflik) left off: that rule-based system handled twenty *balāgha* categories well but explicitly excluded metaphor and periphrasis as too semantically demanding. This paper asks three general-purpose LLMs to do what the rule-based system couldn't, against one expert's (Prof. Hussein's) manual annotation of five classical poems. The headline pattern is that simile — which has an explicit comparative particle to anchor on — is identified reasonably well (strict F1 0.65–0.73), while periphrasis is essentially unreachable (never above 0.176) and antithesis is locatable but wildly over-claimed. The paper frames this as a diagnostic contribution — characterizing *where* LLMs can be trusted on this material — rather than a performance claim, and it is admirably forthright about its own limitations (single annotator, small corpus, worked-example leakage, model drift). The reviewer reads this as a careful, honest piece of applied DH scholarship, well matched to Abd Alhadi et al.'s own genre, but flags that several of its most interesting interpretive claims rest on thinner evidentiary footing than the prose's confidence suggests.

### Five strongest objections

1. **The ground truth is one scholar's judgment, dressed up by a crosswalk the paper does not fully justify.** Data and Methods states the five poems are annotated "against a fifty-three-category classical rhetoric taxonomy, mapped down to the four top-level elements evaluated here by substring matching on the taxonomy's primary and secondary labels (مجاز or استعارة mapping to trope; direct matches for تشبيه, كناية, and طباق)" (Data and Methods, "The corpus and its ground truth" paragraph). A substring-matching crosswalk from a 53-category scheme to 4 buckets is itself an annotation decision with its own error rate, and the paper reports no check on it at all — not even a spot audit. Given that the paper later argues the *trope/periphrasis boundary itself* "turns on whether a literal reading remains plausible, exactly the kind of judgment trained readers of classical Arabic rhetoric can reasonably disagree on" (Limitations, "Ground truth and annotation"), the reviewer wants to know how much of the periphrasis/trope confusion the models exhibit is actually inherited from an unaudited label-mapping step, not from the models at all.

2. **The "zero-shot" claim is compromised by a leakage the paper discloses but does not quantify, and this undermines the headline F1 numbers specifically.** Data and Methods states plainly that "three of every element file's four or five worked examples are, in fact, literal verses from the three TEST poems this paper's primary evaluation scores" (Data and Methods, "The elements and subtypes," final paragraph). This is honestly disclosed, but the paper's own framing — "a held-out-set exposure present in every prompt variant used in this paper, discussed as a limitation below rather than corrected retroactively" — treats it as a closed matter rather than an open threat to the Phase 1 table's validity (Results, "Phase 1: frozen TEST results"). Every number in that table, the paper's single most load-bearing table, was produced under this condition. A reviewer from this background will specifically ask: is the simile ceiling (0.65–0.73) in part an artifact of the model having effectively seen a definitional example *from the verse it is scoring*? Without an estimate of direction and magnitude, this is not a disclosed limitation so much as an unresolved validity threat sitting under the paper's central claim.

3. **The periphrasis "reachability" claim (Abstract, and "Reconciling Phase 1 and Phase 2") is built on a single poem and explicitly described by the paper itself as unstable.** The Abstract states "periphrasis is reachable, though not yet reliably decidable, under a free-form reasoning format" — careful phrasing — but the underlying evidence is Config C's F1 0.111→0.316 gain on Imru2 *only* (Results, "Phase 2: Config A/B/C disentanglement"), which the same section calls "single-poem and preliminary, not a replicated finding," and whose own replication probe caught "4 of 10 verses in at least one of two runs, but only 1 of 10 under this project's own two-run-majority convention" (Results, same section). The reviewer agrees the paper's own hedging language is honest, but questions whether this one-poem, run-unstable result should be elevated to the Abstract and the Conclusion's second sentence at all, given how much interpretive weight ("periphrasis is reachable") a casual reader will place on a sentence that appears twice at the paper's most-read positions.

4. **The AI Disclosure section — required reading for this venue specifically — is incomplete in exactly the three places DHQ's policy makes mandatory.** AI Disclosure, "AI assistance in writing and research," leaves all three substantive disclosure items as `[TBD]`: literature-search assistance, Arabic-terminology translation/glossing assistance, and drafting assistance. DHQ's AI policy (quoted accurately elsewhere in the project's own `dhq_format_guide.md`) requires authors to "acknowledge any substantive use of AI tools in their research and writing process... beyond... routine utilities," and separately imposes zero tolerance for citation inaccuracy with an explicit author-responsibility statement. This paper's own "Accuracy responsibility" paragraph already asserts "[a]ll citations in this paper's Works Cited were verified against the original source... prior to submission" while the three disclosure items above it remain unfilled — an internal inconsistency a DHQ editor is likely to catch at the Step 1 fit-screen, before the paper even reaches peer review.

5. **The corpus's generalizability claim is narrower than some of the Discussion's phrasing implies.** Limitations, "Corpus scope," is appropriately cautious ("Andalusian, Abbasid-era ornate, or Mamluk-era poetry may present a different difficulty profile"), but the Discussion's "What this means for humanities scholars using LLMs on classical Arabic poetry" section gives advice ("a scholar can reasonably treat it as a fast first pass") in a register that reads as addressed to the whole tradition, not to five pre-Islamic/early-Islamic *qaṣīda*s specifically. A reviewer with an Arabic-literature background will want that section's scope restated at its opening, not only in the separate Limitations section several pages later — the paper's own data (one poem with a single antithesis instance, Ground-truth support paragraph in Data and Methods) is too thin a base for tradition-wide practical guidance.

### What this reviewer would need to see to accept

- An explicit statement of how the 53-to-4-category crosswalk was validated (even a small spot-check against Prof. Hussein's own top-level judgment on a sample of verses), or at minimum an acknowledgment of this as an additional, distinct layer of potential error, separate from the existing single-annotator caveat.
- Either a quantified estimate of the worked-example leakage's effect (even on DEV, where a leakage-free prompt variant could be tried without touching TEST), or a visibly stronger hedge on the Phase 1 table's validity in the Abstract itself, not only in a later methods paragraph.
- The periphrasis "reachable" claim moved out of the Abstract/Conclusion's headline position, or qualified in those positions with the same force used in Results ("single-poem and preliminary, not a replicated finding").
- A completed AI Disclosure section — this is close to a hard requirement for this venue, not a style preference.

### Recommendation: **Major revision**

The underlying research design is sound and the paper's honesty about its own limitations is a real strength — unusually so for this genre. But three of the five objections above (crosswalk validation, leakage quantification, the AI Disclosure gap) are things the paper's own evidence base, not just its prose, needs to address before the central claims can be fully trusted. None requires restarting the project; all are tractable extensions or disclosures.

## Reviewer 2 — Computational linguistics reviewer

### How this reviewer understands the paper

A span-level, difficulty-stratified benchmark of three closed-weight LLMs (plus a fourth in an ablation) on four rhetorical-element categories in classical Arabic poetry, with a genuinely careful evaluation protocol: a DEV/TEST split with TEST spent exactly once, two independent span-matching criteria (strict and token-overlap), a 2-run-majority primary metric adopted after an explicit noise incident, and raw TP/FP/FN reported alongside every P/R/F1 figure. The headline finding is a differentiated difficulty pattern across elements (simile easy, periphrasis hard, antithesis over-claimed rather than under-detected) plus two supplementary studies (a model/terminology/format disentanglement, and a 25-subtype recall screen) that surface model- and prompt-dependent instability as a finding in its own right. The reviewer appreciates the methodological self-awareness (the paper catches and reports its own single-run noise incident, its own verse-vs-span scoring artifact, and its own subtype-reversal-at-scale) but reads several of the Discussion's strongest claims as outrunning what the reported statistics actually license.

### Five strongest objections

1. **No significance testing or confidence intervals are reported anywhere, despite denominators small enough that this matters a great deal.** The raw-count row for periphrasis under GPT-5.4-mini is TP=4, FP=7, FN=143 (Results, "Phase 1: frozen TEST results," raw TP/FP/FN paragraph) — an F1 of 0.051 built on 4 true positives. The paper is explicit elsewhere that small-count F1 is unreliable ("an early single-run comparison showed one model's periphrasis F1 apparently increasing eightfold under a candidate prompt rule; a second run brought the honest, majority-vote figure down to roughly a quarter of the apparent gain," Data and Methods, "Span-level scoring") — this is exactly the right instinct — but the fix applied (2-run majority) is a point estimate, not an uncertainty estimate, and the paper draws model-ranking conclusions (e.g., "all three show periphrasis hardest and simile easiest," Discussion, "Cross-model consistency and idiosyncrasy") from differences that, at these counts, plausibly fall inside sampling noise the paper has not bounded.

2. **The subtype-level "model-specific collapse" claims in Discussion are built entirely on a design the paper itself says cannot support them.** Results, "Phase 3: twenty-five-subtype recall screen," states outright that "false positives, true negatives, precision, and F1 are mathematically undefined by this design, not merely unmeasured" — recall-only, on items pre-selected as positive. Yet Discussion, "Prompt sensitivity as a finding, not only a limitation," builds a load-bearing argument on exactly these recall numbers: كناية لغوية "is Gemini 3.5 Flash's strongest subtype... and GPT-5.4-mini's near-total failure on the identical lines," described as "a model-specific collapse, not single-run noise." Recall alone cannot distinguish a genuine capability gap from a shift in a model's *decision threshold* (a model that recalls less on this subtype while also producing far fewer false positives elsewhere would look identical on this metric alone, and would not be "failing" in the sense the prose implies). Without precision, "collapse" is overstated; "a large recall difference under a recall-only design" is the claim the data actually supports.

3. **The majority-vote ensemble finding is reported as a flat fact with no accompanying table or number.** Results, "Trope subtype breakdown," states "[a] majority-vote ensemble across all three models gives no improvement over the best single model for trope or periphrasis... and is worse than the best single model for simile" — but no ensemble F1, TP/FP/FN, or methodology for how the ensemble vote was constructed (unanimity? any-two-of-three? span-level vote aggregation across potentially non-identical span boundaries?) appears anywhere in the paper. This is exactly the kind of claim the paper's own stated discipline ("Always report raw TP/FP/FN alongside precision/recall/F1" — a standing project rule) would normally require, and its absence here is conspicuous against how carefully every other number in the paper is sourced.

4. **Model-version pinning by API string, combined with the complete absence of a fixed seed, is treated as a disclosed limitation rather than a design flaw that could have been mitigated.** Limitations, "Model version drift and corpus rights," is honest that "no guarantee the provider will not update weights behind that identifier," but Data and Methods' "Models" section gives no indication that any provider's seed parameter (where available) was used, and Data and Methods' "Span-level scoring" section's own 2-run-majority protocol exists specifically *because* temperature-0 did not yield deterministic output. A CL reviewer will ask directly: was a seed parameter available from any of the three providers and not used, or unavailable from all three? If available and unused, that is a mitigation the paper should have applied before resorting to a 2-run-majority workaround; if unavailable, the paper should say so explicitly rather than leaving the question open.

5. **The cross-phase comparisons (Phase 1 vs. Phase 3, in particular) pool results from structurally different evaluation designs without flagging the comparison itself as apples-to-oranges at the point where the comparison is made.** The Discussion's "Cross-model consistency and idiosyncrasy" section reads Phase 1's three-model, precision+recall, span-level TEST results against Phase 3's two-model, recall-only, DEV+TEST-pooled subtype screen in the same breath ("[a]t the level of individual subtypes, however, the two-model screen shows GPT-5.4-mini scoring strictly lower than Gemini 3.5 Flash on ten of thirteen reliable subtypes..."), immediately following a sentence about the three-model Phase 1 agreement. The paper's own Limitations section ("Phase 3's recall-only, Gemini-first design") already flags that "every subtype-level claim in this paper is scoped to at most two models for this reason, not three as in the primary evaluation" — but that caveat appears four sections later than where the comparison is actually made, and a reader moving straight through Discussion will not have it in view at the moment it is needed.

### What this reviewer would need to see to accept

- Confidence intervals or a bootstrap/permutation-based significance statement for at least the headline Phase 1 table, given how many of its cells rest on single-digit true-positive counts.
- Either the subtype-level "collapse" language in Discussion softened to match what recall-only data can support, or a negative-sampling pass (already described as "designed but not run" per the project's own planning documents) added for at least the two subtypes the paper leans on most (كناية لغوية, استعارة تمثيلية) before those terms ("collapse," "model-specific") are used.
- The ensemble-vote claim either removed or given the same TP/FP/FN table treatment every other claim in the paper receives, plus a one-sentence description of the voting rule used.
- A direct statement on seed-parameter availability across the three providers, and if available-but-unused, a justification.
- The Phase 1/Phase 3 scope caveat repeated at first use in Discussion, not only in Limitations.

### Recommendation: **Major revision**

The evaluation protocol is genuinely strong by the standards of this sub-field (the paper's own internal methodological corrections — the verse-vs-span artifact catch, the single-run noise incident, the 2-run-majority adoption — are evidence of real statistical care), which makes it more frustrating, not less, that the Discussion periodically outruns what the underlying design can support. These are fixable without new data collection in most cases (significance testing on existing results, softened claim language, documenting the ensemble method) and with a modest, scoped negative-sampling addition in one case.
