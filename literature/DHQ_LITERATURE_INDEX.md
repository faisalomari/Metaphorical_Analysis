# DHQ Literature Index — "Exploring the Potential of LLMs to Identify Challenging Rhetorical Elements"

Compiled 2026-10-07. Scope: *Digital Humanities Quarterly* (DHQ) only. PDFs are in `literature/related_work/dhq/`.
Metadata (title, authors, volume/issue, URL, PDF link) was read directly from each DHQ article page; the PDF first page was checked against the title afterwards.
"Year" = DHQ volume year (as printed on the PDF), which can differ from the page's online publication date.
Relevance scores are my judgment of fit to *this* paper, not of article quality. Descriptions are based on DHQ abstracts and the PDF title pages; Update 2026-10-07: the five original Essential papers were then read in full and notes are in `literature/notes/`; all other descriptions remain abstract-based.

Article URL pattern: `https://dhq.digitalhumanities.org/vol/<vol>/<issue>/<id>/<id>.html`. Official PDF pattern: `https://dhq-static.digitalhumanities.org/pdf/<id>.pdf`.

## Why each matters

The numbering in this section follows the original ranking. The table above is the current ranking (see "Revisions after full read" below).

**1. Abd Alhadi, Hussein & Kuflik (Essential, 10).** Rule-based NLP framework for 20 rhetorical elements of classical Arabic poetry (overall F-measure 0.902), proposed as an interim step until enough annotated text exists to train an ML model. It is the closest DHQ precedent for the task and corpus domain, and the baseline the LLM study must be positioned against. Novelty cannot be "automatic identification of rhetorical elements"; it has to be LLMs, difficulty and expert comparison. Note: the authors are at the University of Haifa, and Dr. Ali Hussein is also the reviewer named in this project's notes.

**2. Gutman (Essential, 9.6).** NLP method (topic classification plus network analysis) for figurative language in *Timon of Athens*. States that figurative language is harder for computation than literal language, and ties the computational result back to close reading. Useful for framing why figurative elements are "challenging" and for a literary-interpretation use of the output.

**3. Münz-Manor & Marienberg-Milikowsky (Essential, 9.3).** Manual and computer-assisted (CATMA) annotation of figures of speech (metaphor, simile, etc.) in Hebrew liturgical poetry, followed by visualisation. Shows that annotation categories get re-assessed after seeing the data. Strong parallel for a Semitic-language poetry corpus and for the annotation and category-validity questions behind a gold standard. This article was not in the earlier deep-research report.

**4. Bender et al. (Essential, 8.8).** Quality criteria for pragmalinguistic tagset development (segment size, category granularity and others) with classification experiments on German parliamentary text. Directly useful for defining rhetorical-element categories and for explaining why a model's score depends on how categories were designed.

**5. Zirker & Göggelmann (Essential, 8.6).** Annotation case study on the ambiguity of "must" in *Emma*: criteria derived from close reading, annotated by students. Gives a model for treating ambiguity and annotator difficulty as something to measure, which supports operationalising "challenging" in the paper.

**6. Bermúdez-Sabel & Dell'Oro (Strong, 7.8).** XML-TEI dataset annotating modal markers, scope and relation in Greek and Latin Gospels. A template for a layered, reusable expert annotation resource and for multilingual annotation design.

**7. Rebora (Strong, 7.6).** Critical survey of sentiment analysis tools in literary studies, with a taxonomy of tools. Useful for the argument that tool output must be validated against humanistic theory and human judgment, not accepted at face value.

**8. Hua & Raley, 2023 (Strong, 7.5).** Maps the code behind GPT-2 and two applications built on it; argues that understanding how large language models work is urgent. DHQ's own LLM-literacy reference, useful for the model-description and reproducibility section (models are not interchangeable black boxes). It concerns GPT-2, not rhetorical analysis.

**9. Dobson (Strong, 7.2).** Warns that accuracy figures, confidence scores and attractive visualisations can be "rhetorically structured" to look more convincing than they are, and sets criteria for interpretable ML in the humanities. Supports a cautious evaluation design.

**10. Tarkka et al. (Strong, 7.2).** Compares a BERT classifier with human questionnaire responses on party affiliation, and uses SHAP to show which cues each relies on; the model picks up personal style and rhetoric that humans miss. A concrete DHQ example of human-versus-machine comparison with explanation analysis.

**11. Gerstorfer, Gius & Jacke (Strong, 7.0).** Editorial for the special issue on categories in text analysis (3 pages). Gives the conceptual vocabulary for category development and operationalisation; use it as a pointer to the other articles in that issue.

**12. Demelas (Strong, 6.8) — PDF not obtained.** Applies a toxicity-detection model to 2,605 sentences of a medieval French epic and finds systematic false negatives, concluding that human-annotated ground truth is needed. Relevant as a recent case of models failing on historical literary language and of the need for expert-annotated ground truth. It is in the 20.3 preview issue.

**13. Riguet & Alrahabi (Strong, 6.5).** Automatic annotation of modal expressions around quotations in 19th-century French literary criticism (EXCOM-2 and E-Quotes). A rule/semantic-category annotation system for a subtle discursive phenomenon. Written in French.

**14. Fuchs (Background, 6.2).** Transformer embeddings (BEREL) and clustering to test inherited genre labels in medieval Hebrew literature. Shows transformer methods applied to a Semitic-language humanities corpus and questioning traditional categories.

**15. Hua & Raley, 2020 (Background, 6.2).** AI Dungeon 2, built on GPT-2, and what its output shows about the model's linguistic competence. Useful background on generative language models as a DHQ subject.

**16. Herrmann et al. (Background, 6.0).** Tool criticism in computational literary studies (method-theory relationship). Supports the methodological stance for evaluating tools critically.

**17. Fan & Presner (Background, 5.8).** Semantic triplets to index and analyse agency in Holocaust testimonies, framed as "algorithmic close reading". Example of connecting automatic extraction to close reading.

**18. McGillivray, Poibeau & Ruiz Fabo (Background, 5.5).** Argues for closer cooperation between NLP and digital humanities. Context for positioning an NLP-style evaluation within DH.

**19–20. Kim and Moro (Background, 5.0 each).** Critiques of sentiment-analysis tools (Syuzhet with its lexicons; TextBlob via critical code studies). Useful only as examples of validating or interrogating an NLP tool applied to literary text.

**21. Herrmann, 2017 (Background, 5.0).** Mixed-method digital stylistics on Kafka, combining quantitative and qualitative analysis. Context for pairing computational output with close reading.

## Revisions after full read (2026-10-07)

Deep-read notes: `literature/notes/` (one per Essential paper). Only two scores changed.

- **Münz-Manor & Marienberg-Milikowsky: 9.3 -> 8.3 (still Essential).** The paper is manual and CATMA-assisted annotation plus visualisation. It has no automatic identification or evaluation of a system, so it is weaker as a methodological match than the abstract suggested. It stays Essential because its figurative-language annotation, and its argument that figurativeness is an interpretive decision, are directly reusable for the paper's "challenging" framing.
- **Zirker & Göggelmann: 8.6 -> 7.4 (Essential -> Strong Candidate).** Manual annotation only, on the modality of "must" rather than rhetorical or figurative elements. Its value is the treatment of ambiguity and annotator disagreement, which supports the paper's difficulty design but is not a rhetorical-element study.
- **Unchanged after the full read:** Abd Alhadi (10), Gutman (9.6), Bender (8.8). Caveat for Gutman: figurative instances were extracted manually, so the automatic part covers topic classification, not detection of figurative language.
- **Added (Part B):** Glass 2026, Background 5.3. It uses CLIP retrieval, not an LLM, on images, so it is not a text or rhetoric paper. Its three-tier difficulty framing (pattern, motif, iconography, with performance falling across tiers) is a methodological analogue for a difficulty taxonomy. Details in `literature/notes/dhq_gap_check.md`.

## How the search was done

- Source list: DHQ's journal-wide title index (887 articles, volumes 1 to 20 including the 20.3 preview issue). Each article's full HTML text was fetched and scanned for term families: rhetoric/figurative (rhetoric, metaphor, simile, metonymy, trope), LLM/GPT/generative AI, ML/NLP, annotation/classification, ambiguity/irony, and Arabic/Hebrew/Persian. Hits were ranked by weighted frequency.
- About 60 top-ranked or title-matched articles were then checked by abstract. 21 were selected. The rest were dropped because the term hits were incidental (for example "rhetoric" or "metaphor" used as a theme in media/rhetoric studies or about DH itself).
- Compared with `deep-research-report.md`: I added Münz-Manor & Marienberg-Milikowsky (figurative-language annotation, Hebrew poetry), Tarkka et al. (human vs model), Demelas (LLM-era toxicity detection), Herrmann et al. and Herrmann 2017, and Hua & Raley 2020. I dropped items I judged weak for this paper (Hongisto on story-generation authorship, Chastang et al. on Latin NER, Argamon & Olsen, Suzuki & Hosoya, Leemans et al.). I did not re-verify its remark about an unpublished GPT-4o German NER manuscript, since it is not a published DHQ article.
- Limitation: keyword screening is not a substitute for reading every article, and the title index may lag the newest issue. No DHQ article found reports a multi-model LLM benchmark on rhetorical-element identification; that is a negative result of this screening, not a claim by DHQ.
