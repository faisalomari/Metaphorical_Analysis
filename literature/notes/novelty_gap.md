# Novelty gap — DHQ + non-DHQ combined (2026-10-07)

This extends `literature/notes/dhq_gap_check.md` (DHQ-only gap check, done in the prior session) with the non-DHQ search done today (task 2 of this session). It states what is already done, what is not, and the exact search scope, so the thesis's novelty claim can be worded accurately.

## What is already done

**LLMs on figurative/metaphorical language identification (non-Arabic, general text).** Several non-DHQ studies evaluate GPT/LLaMA/DeepSeek/Qwen models on metaphor identification or detection:
- Token/word-level, decontextualized: Hicke & Kristensen-McLachlan 2024 (CHR); Reimann & Scheffler 2025 (JLCL, zero/few-shot, largely negative result); Ye et al. 2026 (ACL, diagnostic/skeptical of what high scores mean).
- Full-text, phrase-level, multiple deployment strategies (RAG/prompting/fine-tuning): Fuoli et al. 2025 (arXiv), the most methodologically advanced precedent found, F1 up to 0.79.
- Rhetorical-figure classification specifically (not just "metaphor" as a flat yes/no label): Balestrucci et al. 2025 (CLiC-it) — fine-tunes LLMs to classify 8 named rhetorical figures (analogy, hyperbole, euphemism, rhetorical question, context shift, false assertion, oxymoron/paradox, other) in ironic Italian text, with human-model agreement (Krippendorff's α 0.21–0.26) explicitly measured and compared to human-human agreement (~0.60–0.65).

**LLMs applied to classical/canonical rhetorical analysis specifically.** Two precedents, neither Arabic:
- Ziemińska 2026 (DSH) — Gemini-2.5-Pro for rhetorical analysis of the Book of Revelation, validated against expert judgment (r = 0.82) but found "considerable variability" and "absence of reproducibility." PDF not obtained (paywalled); metadata independently verified.
- (DHQ) Several DHQ annotation studies (Münz-Manor, Zirker, Bender — see `dhq_gap_check.md` and `DHQ_LITERATURE_INDEX.md`) measure inter-annotator agreement on figurative/pragmatic categories, but none use an LLM as the identifying agent.

**LLMs applied to Arabic rhetoric (balagha) specifically.** One precedent: Al Kaabi et al. 2024 (JIPD) — ChatGPT compared against classical scholarly interpretation (al-Jurjani) on 8 hand-picked rhetorical examples (metaphor, simile, metonymy). Qualitative, small-N (8 examples), not a benchmark with precision/recall, no held-out test set, no multiple models. Finds ChatGPT gives surface-level but not culturally-deep interpretations.

**Rule-based / non-LLM automatic identification of rhetorical elements in classical Arabic poetry.** Abd Alhadi, Hussein & Kuflik 2023 (DHQ) — the direct predecessor in domain (same REI poetry corpus, Haifa group), rule-based not LLM-based, explicitly excludes metaphor and periphrasis (kinaya) as "too semantic" for rules. This is the paper our proposed LLM study is positioned to extend.

## What is NOT done (the gap)

No paper found — in DHQ or outside it — does all of the following at once:
1. Evaluates **multiple LLMs** (not just one model/one API call)
2. On **named classical rhetorical elements** (not a flat "metaphor yes/no" label, not irony-specific figures, not modern literary metaphor only)
3. **In classical Arabic poetry** specifically
4. With a **difficulty/ambiguity analysis** (why certain elements are harder — periphrasis/كناية being the known hard case per Abd Alhadi 2023)
5. With an **expert-vs-LLM comparison design** reporting agreement/disagreement with quantified metrics (not just qualitative commentary, as in Al Kaabi et al. 2024)

The closest approximations, and exactly how each falls short:
- **Abd Alhadi et al. 2023**: right domain and right task, but rule-based, not LLM-based, and explicitly brackets out metaphor/periphrasis as the semantics-dependent elements it cannot handle — which is precisely the gap an LLM study could fill.
- **Al Kaabi et al. 2024**: right domain (Arabic rhetoric) and right technology (ChatGPT), but single model, 8 hand-picked examples, qualitative only, no benchmark metrics, no classical-poetry corpus (uses isolated sentences/proverbs, not a poem corpus).
- **Fuoli et al. 2025 / Reimann & Scheffler 2025 / Balestrucci et al. 2025 / Ye et al. 2026**: rigorous multi-model benchmarking methodology with exactly the kind of evaluation design (precision/recall/F1, agreement metrics, error taxonomies, diagnostic probing) this thesis should borrow — but none touch Arabic or classical poetry at all.
- **Ziemińska 2026**: closest in spirit (LLM + rhetorical analysis + expert validation design, with reproducibility concerns flagged) but for a different canonical text (Book of Revelation, in Greek/English scholarship) and a single model (Gemini), not Arabic poetry and not multi-model.

## Verdict

**Gap confirmed**, with the following precise wording suggested for the thesis: *"No existing study evaluates multiple large language models on the identification of named classical Arabic rhetorical elements — including the semantically demanding cases (metaphor, periphrasis) that rule-based systems (Abd Alhadi et al. 2023) explicitly could not handle — against expert-annotated ground truth with a quantified, difficulty-stratified evaluation. The closest precedents are: a rule-based (non-LLM) system in the same domain (Abd Alhadi et al. 2023); a single-model, small-N, qualitative LLM study of Arabic rhetoric outside a poetry corpus (Al Kaabi et al. 2024); and rigorous multi-model LLM benchmarking methodologies for metaphor/figurative language in other languages with no Arabic or classical-poetry application (Fuoli et al. 2025; Reimann & Scheffler 2025; Balestrucci et al. 2025)."*

This is worded as a **screening result**, not an exhaustive claim: it reflects the venues and search terms listed above, not a literature review of every NLP or digital-humanities venue that could conceivably contain a counterexample.

## Update (2026-10-07, task 2 of this session) — Kinayat (Attia et al. 2026) and the Kühn et al. rhetorical-figures papers

Three new papers were deep-read this session: Attia, Muhamed, Alkhamissi, Solorio & Diab (2026) "Beyond Understanding" / Kinayat (EACL 2026), and two Kühn-et-al. papers on computational detection of lesser-known rhetorical figures (Kühn, Mitrović & Granitzer 2024, systematic survey; Kühn & Mitrović 2024, FigLang short paper). Full notes: `2026_Attia_KinayatPragmaticGapFigurativeLanguage.md`, `2024_Kuhn_LesserKnownRhetoricalFiguresSurvey.md`, `2024_Kuhn_ElephantRoomTenChallenges.md`.

### What Kinayat (Attia et al. 2026) already covers — and what it narrows in our claim

Kinayat is the single closest precedent found so far to the "multi-model LLM evaluation of Arabic figurative language" building block of our novelty claim. It is **more rigorous than Al Kaabi et al. 2024** on that specific axis: 22 open- and closed-source LLMs (vs. Al Kaabi's 1), a purpose-built dataset of 325 Egyptian Arabic idioms plus existing multidialectal-proverb and English-proverb datasets (vs. 8 hand-picked examples), and a quantified benchmark design (accuracy, standard error, a novel "Pragmatics Gap" metric, human-agreement figures) rather than qualitative commentary. This means **component 1 of our gap statement — "evaluates multiple LLMs" — and component 5 — "expert-vs-LLM comparison with quantified metrics" — are no longer unique to a hypothetical future study**: Kinayat already does both, for a different slice of Arabic figurative language.

What Kinayat does **not** do, and where our gap remains fully open:
- **Not classical poetry.** Kinayat uses colloquial Egyptian idioms and MSA/multidialectal proverbs — modern, everyday figurative expressions, not verses from classical Arabic poetry (pre-Islamic through Mamluk, per Abd Alhadi et al.'s corpus).
- **Not named classical rhetorical elements.** Kinayat has no taxonomy of antithesis/طباق, periphrasis/كناية, metaphor/استعارة, or metonymy/مجاز مرسل — it tests "idiom/proverb understanding, pragmatic use, and connotation," a cultural/pragmatic-competence framing, not a rhetorical-figure-identification framing. Despite the dataset's name ("Kinayat," borrowed loosely from كناية in the sense of "indirect/allusive expression" generally, not the specific classical balagha category), it is not a periphrasis-detection study and should not be cited as one.
- **No difficulty-stratification by rhetorical-element type.** Kinayat stratifies difficulty by language (English vs. Arabic), register (MSA proverb vs. colloquial idiom), and task type (understanding vs. pragmatic use) — not by which named rhetorical category is hardest, which is this project's central empirical question.
- **No span-level identification task.** Kinayat's tasks are all closed-form (MCQ, fill-in-the-blank, connotation classification) — it never asks a model to locate/identify a rhetorical device within a running text, which is the identification task Abd Alhadi et al. 2023 and this project both center on.

**Revised wording for component 3 of the gap** ("in classical Arabic poetry specifically") stands unchanged and sharpens further: Kinayat is explicit that classical/literary Arabic figurative language is out of its scope, naming "Fann or Flop" (Alghallabi et al. 2025, an Arabic-poetry-interpretation benchmark, not rhetorical-figure identification) as the only adjacent work in its own related-work table (Table 2) — reinforcing, not weakening, that no benchmark combines LLMs + classical Arabic poetry + named rhetorical elements.

### What the Kühn et al. papers add — and what they do not

Both Kühn et al. papers are general (non-Arabic) surveys of computational rhetorical-figure detection, covering antithesis, metonymy, litotes, oxymoron, chiasmus, zeugma, and 20+ other "lesser-known" figures across 39 papers / 86 approaches, almost entirely in English, German, Russian, French, Latin, Chinese, Czech, and Japanese. Neither paper includes a single Arabic-language approach, and neither covers periphrasis/كناية at all (it is absent from both papers' complete figure lists). They support the background claim that **named, non-metaphor/irony rhetorical figures are a generally under-studied, rule-based-dominated sub-field** (68.97% of 86 surveyed approaches are rule-based; only 1 combines rule- and model-based methods; no approach anywhere uses the existing rhetorical-figure ontologies) — a useful structural analogy for why periphrasis and antithesis specifically resisted Abd Alhadi et al.'s 2023 rule-based system, but they add **no direct evidence about Arabic or about periphrasis itself**. Their antithesis-detection chain (best non-Arabic result: Kühn et al. 2024's own rule-+-model-based German pipeline, F1 65.11%) is a useful non-Arabic comparison point for antithesis detection performance, not a gap-narrowing precedent.

### Revised gap statement (replaces the 2026-10-07 wording above for any writing from this point forward)

*"No existing study evaluates multiple large language models on the identification of named classical rhetorical elements in classical Arabic poetry — including the semantically demanding cases (metaphor, periphrasis) that rule-based systems (Abd Alhadi et al. 2023) explicitly could not handle — against expert-annotated ground truth with a span-level, difficulty-stratified evaluation. This gap sits at the intersection of three literatures that are each individually active but have not been combined: (1) multi-model LLM benchmarking of Arabic figurative/cultural language, now including a rigorous recent precedent (Attia et al. 2026 / Kinayat, 22 models, quantified human-agreement metrics) — but applied to modern colloquial idioms and proverbs, not classical poetry or named rhetorical categories; (2) a general computational-rhetoric literature on detecting named, lesser-known rhetorical figures (Kühn, Mitrović & Granitzer 2024; Kühn & Mitrović 2024) — but with no Arabic-language work and no treatment of periphrasis/كناية at all; and (3) a single rule-based system in exactly our domain (Abd Alhadi et al. 2023) that explicitly excludes the semantically demanding elements an LLM-based approach is best positioned to address."*

**Plain statement of what narrowed**: the claim that "no study evaluates multiple LLMs on Arabic figurative language" must be narrowed — Attia et al. 2026 does this, rigorously. What survives unnarrowed is the more specific and still-load-bearing claim: no study evaluates multiple LLMs on **named classical rhetorical elements** in **classical Arabic poetry** with **difficulty stratification by element**. This is in fact a *stronger* framing for the thesis than the pre-session wording, since it can now cite a recent, rigorous multi-model Arabic-figurative-language precedent to show the field has the methodological tools (Attia et al.'s task design, human-agreement reporting) but has not pointed them at classical poetry or named balagha categories.
