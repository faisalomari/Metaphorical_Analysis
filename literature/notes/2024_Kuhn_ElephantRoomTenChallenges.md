# Kühn & Mitrović (2024) — The Elephant in the Room: Ten Challenges of Computational Detection of Rhetorical Figures

Proceedings of the 4th Workshop on Figurative Language Processing (FigLang 2024), co-located with NAACL 2024, pp. 45–52. https://aclanthology.org/2024.figlang-1.6/ (DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.figlang-1.6) — PDF: `literature/related_work/non_dhq/2024_Kuhn_ElephantRoomTenChallenges.pdf`
Read: full paper (short paper, 8 pages incl. references/appendix). Page refs below are PDF pages. "Not reported" means not found in the paper.

## Task / scope
- Not a detection system — a short survey/position paper distilling ten recurring challenges from a review of 40+ papers (2006–2024) on computational detection of rhetorical figures **other than** metaphor, simile, analogy, sarcasm, and irony (p.1). Companion short paper to the longer systematic survey (Kühn, Mitrović & Granitzer 2024, `2024_Kuhn_LesserKnownRhetoricalFiguresSurvey.md`) by largely the same authors — this paper distills the same underlying 40+ paper review into a crisper 10-item list plus suggested solutions for each.
- Search method: Google Scholar, figure name + "detection"/"identification", explicitly not restricted to ACL Anthology since the field is under-represented at major NLP venues (p.1).
- Scope restricted to written-text detection (speech/multimodal excluded) (p.1).

## The ten challenges (Section 2, p.2–5)
1. **Inconsistent definitions and binary classification** — figures like antithesis lack a single agreed definition (e.g., whether syntactic parallelism is required); some figures don't translate across languages (metaphor+simile merge into one figure in Chinese). Suggested fix: consult multiple sources, treat detection as a ranking/salience task (continuous values) rather than binary present/absent, using comparison-based (best-worst-scaling) annotation to keep reliability up.
2. **Defining boundaries and intentional usage** — figures can span sentences/paragraphs; hard to decide where a figure starts/ends, and whether an apparent repetition is rhetorically intentional or accidental. Strommer (2011): annotators agreed on only 2 of 156 candidate anaphora instances as truly intentional. Fix: use larger text chunks in dataset construction, apply the same salience-ranking approach as challenge 1.
3. **Lack of data/datasets** — popular figures benefit from social-media self-tagging (e.g., #sarcasm); lesser-known figures don't, and datasets are biased toward only the most salient, clear-cut instances (excluding genuinely arguable edge cases). Fix: LLM-assisted sentence generation (with the caveat that an LLM can only generate figures it already "knows," risking a circularity/vicious-cycle problem), human-verified; gamified data-collection platforms.
4. **Imbalanced datasets and deceptive performance metrics** — in a dataset where 99% of instances are not the figure, a trivial always-negative classifier scores 99% accuracy. Fix: augmentation/over- or under-sampling; choose metrics (P/R/F1) carefully, not just accuracy.
5. **Not including ontologies** — formal rhetorical-figure ontologies exist (English RhetFig, Ploke, Serbian Retfig, German GRhOOT, a multilingual ontology) but **none of the 40+ reviewed detection approaches actually uses them**. Fix: integrate ontologies into detection pipelines, especially via RAG with LLMs.
6. **Missing context** — figures are often only interpretable with surrounding context; some (rhetorical questions, hyperbole) are syntactically identical to their literal counterparts and only distinguishable by context (worked example: "It took ages to build the castle" is literal for a real castle, hyperbolic for a child's sandcastle). Fix: include preceding/following sentences in the detection window; use LLMs to resolve contextual ambiguity.
7. **Focus on rule-based methods** — of 87 detection techniques identified across the reviewed papers, 68.97% are rule-based, only 27.59% model-based, just 1 approach (Kühn et al. 2024, the authors' own antithesis paper) combines both (Table 1, p.4). Zhu et al. (2022) found rule-based methods underperform because the task can't be solved by "identifying shallow and obvious patterns." Fix: adopt LLM-based approaches, ideally combined with rules for figures with strict lexical-repetition components.
8. **Focus on English** — 69.81% of the 86 counted approaches are English-only (Table 2, p.4); direct translation of a figure into English loses syntactic form (worked German example: separable verbs like "an...denken" create spurious lexical repetition); English's lack of grammatical gender/inflection makes certain figures (e.g., polyptoton, which depends on inflectional variation) rarer than in more inflected languages like German. This English focus is self-reinforcing (#BenderRule) and correlates with lower NLP-conference acceptance for non-English papers (Søgaard 2022). Fix: no easy fix — reward and fund non-English rhetorical-figure research, build adequate tools in more languages.
9. **Neglecting cognitive effects** — detection research mostly stops at "is the figure present," rarely analyzing *why* it's used or what emotional/persuasive effect it creates in context. Fix: pair detection with explanation of function/effect.
10. **Lack of interdisciplinary efforts** — rhetorical figures touch syntax, semantics, sound, negation, neuroscience, law, biology/chemistry communication, environmental-policy argumentation; NLP researchers rarely collaborate across these. Fix: cross-disciplinary teams (computer scientists + linguists + domain experts).

## Reported quantitative facts (useful as citable numbers)
- 68.97% rule-based vs. 27.59% model-based vs. 1.15% combined vs. 2.30% unknown, across 86 counted approaches from 40+ papers (Table 1, p.4).
- 69.81% of approaches target English; next highest is German at 9.43%, then Russian 7.55% (Table 2, p.4).
- Silva Rhetoricae (an online rhetorical-figures resource) lists 435 distinct rhetorical figures total — most far less studied computationally than metaphor (p.1).

## Difficulties / limitations (as a survey, not an empirical study)
- The paper itself has no dataset, no experiments, no performance metrics — it is a structured literature synthesis plus a suggested-solutions list. Treat its "findings" as a secondary-source diagnosis of the field, not primary empirical results.
- Figure coverage: the survey explicitly excludes metaphor, simile, analogy, sarcasm, irony — i.e., it is deliberately about everything else, which happens to include antithesis but not periphrasis/كناية or metaphor/metonymy by name (those fall under the excluded "popular figures" category or are not covered at all, since the underlying figures are English/German/etc.-centric, not Arabic balagha-specific).

## I notice
- Antithesis is explicitly named as an example figure suffering from challenge 1 (inconsistent definitions, esp. whether syntactic parallelism is mandatory) — directly parallels this project's own antithesis/طباق definitional questions.
- The explicit finding that **no reviewed approach uses the rhetorical-figure ontologies that exist** for the field is a clean, quotable fact supporting a "the field under-resources named, non-metaphor rhetorical figures" framing — but note this paper and its companion survey do not address Arabic or periphrasis/كناية at all; their "lesser-known figures" are drawn from the Anglo/European rhetorical tradition (antithesis, chiasmus, zeugma, litotes, etc.), not from balagha.
- Caution: periphrasis (كناية) does not appear anywhere in this paper's figure list or its companion survey's 26-figure table — the "periphrasis is a known hard case for rule-based systems" claim in our novelty_gap.md rests on Abd Alhadi et al. 2023, not on this paper. Do not overstate this paper as directly validating periphrasis-specific difficulty; it supports the broader, structurally analogous claim that under-studied non-metaphor rhetorical figures are harder (inconsistent definitions, scarce data, context-dependence), which periphrasis also exhibits.

## Reusable ideas for our paper
- The ten-challenge checklist is a ready-made framework to map our own project's difficulty findings against in the Discussion section (e.g., "challenge 1, inconsistent definitions" maps onto our periphrasis/antithesis definitional ambiguity; "challenge 3/4, data scarcity and imbalance" maps onto our small DEV/TEST sizes per element).
- The "detection should be a ranking/salience task, not binary" suggestion is a reusable framing device if we ever discuss graded/partial correctness in LLM outputs rather than strict present/absent spans.
- The explicit English-language dominance critique (challenge 8) is directly citable to frame why Arabic rhetorical-element identification is itself an under-served niche within an already under-served sub-field (non-metaphor rhetorical figures).
